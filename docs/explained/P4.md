# Step P4 — Analytics: dwell time, heatmap and alert debouncing (explained for a beginner)

This note explains every file and function written in step P4, in plain English, with a tiny
number example for each idea. Read it once, then explain it out loud in your own words.

---

## 1. What this step does, in five sentences

1. P3 could answer *"how many people are in this zone right now?"*. P4 answers three questions
   that need **memory over time**.
2. **`DwellTracker`** answers *"how long has each person been standing here?"* by remembering
   when every track ID entered every zone.
3. **`HeatmapAccumulator`** answers *"where do crowds keep forming?"* by piling up foot points
   into a picture that slowly fades.
4. **`AlertDebouncer`** answers *"is this zone really full, or did the count just flicker?"* by
   requiring the problem to last `ON_DELAY` seconds before alerting.
5. `scripts/demo_step4.py` runs all three on a real clip and writes a video where you can see
   the heat, the dwell numbers and every alert with its timestamp.

**One rule that touches everything in this file:** all times come from the **video timestamp**
(`frame_index / fps`). Nothing calls `time.time()`. A recording being replayed is not happening
now — if the CPU takes 4 minutes to process a 45-second clip, the alert at video second 31.5 must
still say 31.5, not 240.

---

## 2. `app/config.py` — three new settings

| Setting | Value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `HEAT_BLUR_FRACTION` | `0.05` | Blur width as a fraction of frame width; 0.05 × 640 = a 33 px kernel |
| `HEAT_MASK_RATIO` | `0.08` | A pixel is drawn only if it has ≥ 8% of the hottest pixel's heat |
| `HEAT_OVERLAY_ALPHA` | `0.5` | Half video, half heat colour, where the mask is true |

Existing settings used by this step: `HEAT_DECAY = 0.995`, `TRACK_GRACE = 2.0`,
`ON_DELAY = 3.0`, `OFF_DELAY = 5.0`.

**Why these live in config:** they are the numbers you would want to change on stage
("let's make the heat fade faster"), and changing behaviour should never mean editing logic.

## 3. `app/analytics.py` — the three classes

### 3.1 `_odd(value)` — a tiny guard

Gaussian blur only accepts an **odd** kernel size. Asking for 32 raises an error, which would
crash a demo halfway through a clip.

```python
_odd(32) -> 33
_odd(33) -> 33
_odd(34) -> 35
```

**Why a named function instead of `| 1` inline:** the next reader has to be told *why* the
number must be odd, and a function with a docstring is the natural place for that.

### 3.2 `DwellTracker`

#### The idea

For every `(zone, track_id)` pair we store two times:

```python
_DwellEntry(entered_at=9.0, last_seen=12.0)
```

- `entered_at` → "how long have they been here?" (`now - entered_at`)
- `last_seen` → "are they still here, or have they gone?" (drives forgetting)

**Why the pair is `(zone, id)` and not just `id`:** a person can walk from a quiet zone into a
busy one. The second zone's dwell must start when they cross the line, not when they first
appeared on screen. Otherwise a person who spent 10 s in Zone A and 2 s in Zone B would show
12 s in Zone B, which is simply false.

#### `update(now, members)`

```python
members = {"Main Zone": [7, 9]}
```

Three things happen, in this order:

1. **New IDs start their clock.**
   `(Main Zone, 7)` not in the dict → store `entered_at = now`.
2. **Returning IDs only refresh `last_seen`.**
   The entry time is *never* rewritten. This is the important line: a person the detector loses
   for one frame must not have their dwell time reset to zero. A person standing still is the
   easiest person to lose for a frame — and the hardest to re-detect as "new" — so they are
   exactly the person whose dwell must survive.
3. **Old IDs are dropped.**
   ```python
   if now - entry.last_seen <= config.TRACK_GRACE
   ```
   With `TRACK_GRACE = 2.0`: someone last seen at 12.0 s is still remembered at 13.9 s and is
   gone at 14.1 s.

**Why a grace window at all:** without it, one missed detection would end a visit and a genuine
10-second wait would be recorded as two 4-second waits. The average would be wrong and the
maximum would never show the real bottleneck.

#### `stats(zone_name, now)` → `(average, maximum)`

```python
durations = [now - entry.entered_at for entries in that zone]
return (sum(durations)/len(durations), max(durations))   # or (0.0, 0.0) if empty
```

**Why two numbers and not one:** an average hides the bottleneck.

| visitors | durations | average | maximum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 2 s, 2 s, 2 s, 60 s | 16.5 s | **60 s** |
| B | 8 s, 8 s, 8 s, 8 s | 8.0 s | 8 s |

Zone A looks *worse* on the average, but zone B is uniformly crowded. More importantly, zone A's
maximum says "one person is genuinely stuck in a doorway" — which is the bottleneck question this
project is about. The average alone cannot say that.

**Why `(0, 0)` and not `None`:** the dashboard and the demo can then format the number
unconditionally (`f"{avg:.1f}s"`) with no `if` branch, and no test has to special-case empty.

**Worked example from the spec:** ID 7 enters at `9.0`, asked at `12.4`:

```
12.4 - 9.0 = 3.4   ->  average 3.4, maximum 3.4
```

#### `reset()`

Clears everything. **Why:** the next clip has different track IDs. Without a reset, clip 2 would
inherit clip 1's entries and report dwell times measured in a different video.

### 3.3 `HeatmapAccumulator`

#### The idea

A heatmap is a 2D grid of numbers, one per pixel, that grows where feet land. Draw it as colour
and the warmest area is literally "where people spend their time".

#### `__init__(width, height)`

```python
self.heat = np.zeros((height, width), dtype=np.float32)
```

**Why `float32` and not `uint8`:** the decay multiplies by 0.995. In an integer array,
`255 * 0.995 = 253.7` truncates to 253, then 251, then 250 — the heat would fall off a cliff in
a few frames and old spots would vanish instantly. A float keeps `0.995, 0.990, 0.985 …` honestly.

**Numeric example:** a pixel at 1.0 with `HEAT_DECAY = 0.995` after 100 frames:
`0.995 ** 100 ≈ 0.606`. In `uint8` scaled to 255 that would be 255 → 154, then `0.995` applied
to 154 gives 153.2 → **153**, and the error compounds every frame.

**Why `(height, width)` on the array but `width, height` in the signature:** numpy shape is
rows-then-columns, i.e. height-then-width. The constructor takes them in the human order
("how wide, how tall") and converts once.

#### `add(points)`

```python
self.heat *= config.HEAT_DECAY          # 1. fade everyone a little
for x, y in points:                     # 2. clamp, then add 1.0
    self.heat[clamped_y, clamped_x] += 1.0
```

**Why fade first, then add:** this makes the newest frame count fully and older frames count
less. Fading *after* adding would slightly shrink the point you just added, which is harmless but
confusing to explain.

**Why clamp instead of skip:** a tracker occasionally reports a foot one pixel outside the frame
(`x = -3`). Skipping it would silently lose a person — a busy frame would look emptier than it
was. Clamping to `0` keeps the evidence and cannot raise an `IndexError`.

**Numeric example:** a pixel at 1.0 with one new foot point on it:
`1.0 * 0.995 + 1.0 = 1.995`.

**Why the decay exists at all:** without it, one crowded minute would glow for the rest of the
clip and the heatmap would stop describing the present. The heatmap is supposed to answer "where
is it busy *now-ish*", not "where has anyone ever stood".

#### `layer()` → `(colour_image, mask)`

Five steps:

1. **Empty guard.** `peak <= 0` → return a black image and an all-false mask. Dividing by zero
   would give `NaN`, then `NaN` cast to `uint8` gives garbage colours — a bug that only appears
   at the very start of a clip.
2. **Blur.** `cv2.GaussianBlur(heat, (k, k), 0)` with `k = odd(width * 0.05)`.
   *Why:* one foot point is a **single pixel** — completely invisible in a video. Blur turns it
   into a soft blob, and neighbouring blobs merge into a visible hot area.
3. **Normalise.** `blurred / blurred_peak`, clipped to `0..1`, × 255.
   *Why:* raw heat can be 2 early in a clip and 200 later. Dividing by the current peak makes the
   colours mean "hot **compared with now**", which is what a viewer expects. It also means the
   picture looks the same whether the clip is 10 seconds or 10 minutes old.
4. **Colour.** `cv2.applyColorMap(grey, cv2.COLORMAP_JET)` → blue (cold) to red (hot).
5. **Mask.** `normalised >= HEAT_MASK_RATIO` (0.08).
   *Why 8% and not 0:* without a mask the **whole frame** is tinted blue, including empty
   pavement, and the video underneath becomes hard to read. The mask says "only paint where there
   is real heat".

**Numeric example:** raw values `0.0, 0.5, 2.0` with peak `2.0` normalise to `0, 64, 255`.
`64 >= 0.08 * 255 = 20.4`, so both the middle and the top pixel are painted; the zero pixel is not.

**Why return a tuple and not a combined image:** `overlay()` needs the mask separately to blend
only where it is true. Returning a pre-blended picture would force the caller to re-derive the
mask, which is exactly the kind of duplication that drifts.

#### `overlay(frame, alpha=0.5)` → new frame

```python
blended = frame.copy()
blended[mask] = frame[mask] * (1 - alpha) + colour[mask] * alpha
return blended
```

**Why only inside the mask:** outside it the video is byte-for-byte untouched, so people and zone
borders stay sharp. Heat reads as an *extra layer*, not as a filter over everything.

**Why a copy:** the caller still needs the clean frame for detection and for saving. Painting
into it would feed our own colours back into the next frame's detector.

**Numeric example:** frame pixel `(100, 100, 100)` under a red heat pixel `(0, 0, 255)` with
`alpha = 0.5` → `(50, 50, 178)`.

**Why `np.where`/boolean indexing and not `cv2.addWeighted`:** `addWeighted` blends the **whole**
image. We want a per-pixel decision, and a boolean mask on a numpy array does exactly that in one
line.

#### `reset()`

`self.heat[:] = 0.0`. **Why:** carrying heat between clips would draw hot spots in places nobody
has stood in the new video.

### 3.4 `AlertDebouncer`

#### Why this class exists (the whole point)

Look at the real P3 log for `mid.mp4`:

```
    12.3s  Main Zone  5/5  OK -> NEAR
    12.3s  Main Zone  6/5  NEAR -> OVER      <- over
    12.4s  Main Zone  5/5  OVER -> NEAR      <- back under, 0.1 s later
    12.5s  Main Zone  6/5  NEAR -> OVER      <- over again
    12.7s  Main Zone  4/5  OVER -> NEAR
```

The raw count crosses the line **five times in one second**. If "count > capacity" raised an
alert, the operator would get five alerts for one event and stop reading them. A **debouncer** is
the standard fix: require the condition to hold for a while before believing it.

#### State

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `active` | is an alert currently open? (public) |
| `_over_since` | when the current over-capacity run started, or `None` |
| `_under_since` | when the current at-or-under run started, or `None` |
| `_last_now` | the last timestamp seen, so `over_for` can answer with no argument |

Only one of `_over_since` / `_under_since` is ever set, because the count is either over
capacity or not — there is no third state.

#### `update(now, count, capacity)` → `"TRIGGERED" | "CLEARED" | None`

```
if count > capacity:
    if _over_since is None: _over_since = now       # start a new run
    _under_since = None                             # the under run is dead
    if not active and now - _over_since >= ON_DELAY:
        active = True
        return "TRIGGERED"
    return None

else:
    if _under_since is None: _under_since = now
    _over_since = None
    if active and now - _under_since >= OFF_DELAY:
        active = False
        return "CLEARED"
    return None
```

**Why remember the run's start time instead of adding up `now - previous_now`:**
two reasons.
1. If the caller skips frames (STRIDE, or a slow CPU dropping a frame), summing deltas loses the
   gap and the delay fires late.
2. `now - run_start` is **idempotent**: calling `update` twice with the same timestamp cannot
   double-count. Summing deltas would add the interval twice.

**Why `>=` and not `>`:** "over for 3.0 seconds" should fire *at* 3.0, not at 3.03. With
samples 0.5 s apart, `>=` fires at exactly the 3.0 sample; `>` would fire at 3.5.

**Why `not active` in the trigger test:** the alert is a statement about an **episode**. It
fires once when the episode starts and stays quiet for the rest of it, however many more frames
are over capacity.

**Why `active` in the clear test:** a debouncer that never triggered has nothing to clear. Without
that guard, every quiet frame after `OFF_DELAY` would return `CLEARED`, filling the log with
noise.

**Worked example (the exact test in the file):** capacity 5, `ON_DELAY = 3.0`.

| video time | count | run started | event |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0 | 6 | 0.0 | None |
| 1.0 | 6 | 0.0 | None (1.0 < 3.0) |
| 2.0 | 6 | 0.0 | None (2.0 < 3.0) |
| **3.0** | 6 | 0.0 | **TRIGGERED** (3.0 ≥ 3.0) |
| 3.5 | 6 | 0.0 | None (already active) |
| 4.0 | 6 | 0.0 | None (already active) |

**Worked example of a flicker being ignored:** `6` at 0.0, 0.5, 1.0 then `5` at 1.5.
The run reaches 1.0 s, then the under-capacity frame wipes `_over_since`. Nothing fires. This is
the "don't cry wolf" behaviour, and it is a test.

#### `over_for` (a property)

```python
return 0.0 if _over_since is None else max(0.0, _last_now - _over_since)
```

**Why expose it:** the dashboard shows "over for 4.1 s", which tells an operator whether an alert
is about to fire — the count alone does not. It also makes the demo's printed summary honest.

**Why `max(0.0, ...)`:** defensive. If a caller ever passes a timestamp that goes slightly
backwards, this returns 0 instead of a negative number that would look absurd on screen.

## 5. `scripts/demo_step4.py` — the visible proof

```powershell
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\demo_step4.py demo_videos\crowded.mp4
```

Per analysed frame: detect → group feet by zone → feed `DwellTracker` → feed
`HeatmapAccumulator` → feed each zone's `AlertDebouncer` → draw → write.

### 5.1 Draw order (deliberate)

```python
canvas = heatmap.overlay(frame)      # 1. heat on the bottom
draw_people(canvas, people)          # 2. boxes + foot dots
canvas = draw_zones(canvas, zones, counts)   # 3. translucent zone fill + label
draw_dwell_labels(...)               # 4. dwell numbers
draw_status_strip(...)               # 5. top summary
draw_events(...)                     # 6. alert banner
```

**Why the heat goes first:** it is the *background layer*. Drawing it last would tint the box
borders and the zone outline, and a red box under a red heat blob becomes invisible.

### 5.2 `draw_events` — why a 4-second hold

`EVENT_HOLD_SECONDS = 4.0` keeps an event line on screen for four video seconds. **Why:** an
event that flashes for a single frame is unreadable in a video, and the video is the evidence a
reviewer will actually look at. `MAX_EVENT_LINES = 4` stops the panel from covering the whole
picture.

### 5.3 The playback-rate fix (a real bug this step found)

The first render produced a 674-frame file at 29.97 fps → **22.49 s** for a 44.98 s clip. The
video played at **double speed**, and the printed "31.5 s TRIGGERED" could not be found in the
file at 31.5 s, because the file ended at 22.5 s.

The cause: we write **one frame per `STRIDE` (2) source frames**, but told the writer to use the
*source* frame rate. Fix:

```python
writer = open_writer(destination, canvas, fps / config.STRIDE)   # 29.97 / 2 = 14.98
```

A 674-frame file at 14.98 fps is **44.98 s** — exactly the source length. Every drawn timestamp
now matches the player's clock, which is the whole point of printing video times.

**The same bug existed in `demo_step3.py`**, so I fixed it there too and re-rendered those videos.
`demo_step1.py` is left alone: its overlay shows measured *processing* FPS, not video timestamps,
and P4 was not asked to change P1's behaviour.

Verified durations after the fix:

| file | duration |
| --- | --- |
| `demo_videos/crowded.mp4` | 44.98 s |
| `outputs/step4_crowded.mp4` | **44.98 s** |
| `outputs/step4_mid.mp4` | 31.16 s (source 31.13 s) |
| `outputs/step4_normal.mp4` | 24.48 s (source 24.44 s) |

## 7. The three most important lines in this step

1. **`if now - entry.last_seen <= config.TRACK_GRACE`**
   The grace window is what makes dwell time survive a missed detection. Without it, the person
   standing most still — the bottleneck — would have their timer reset constantly.

2. **`if not self.active and now - self._over_since >= config.ON_DELAY`**
   Three guards in one line: an episode fires once (`not active`), only after a real duration
   (`>= ON_DELAY`), measured in **video** seconds. Change any of them and the alert becomes
   either a spam machine or a silent one.

3. **`self.heat *= config.HEAT_DECAY`**
   The one line that makes the heatmap describe the *recent* past. Without it, the first crowded
   minute would glow for the whole clip and the picture would answer "where has anybody ever
   stood?" instead of "where is it busy?".

## 8. What could go wrong (and the fix)

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alert fires on the wrong frame | `now` came from `time.time()` | Always pass `frame_index / fps` |
| Alert never fires | `ON_DELAY` longer than the over-capacity run | Lower `ON_DELAY`, or check the count really stays over |
| Alert fires every frame | `active` guard removed | Keep `not self.active` in the trigger test |
| Dwell time keeps climbing after someone leaves | `TRACK_GRACE` too large, or `last_seen` not updated | Check `update()` is called every analysed frame |
| Whole frame tinted blue | mask removed or `HEAT_MASK_RATIO` set to 0 | Restore the mask; raise the ratio |
| Heatmap looks frozen | `HEAT_DECAY` set to 1.0 | Lower it (0.995 is the shipped value) |
| Heat visible but no colours | all values normalised to 0 | The map was empty — check `add()` is being called |
| Video plays at double speed | writer created with the source fps | Use `fps / config.STRIDE` (fixed in this step) |
| `Cannot write video` | a player is holding the old MP4 | Close the player and re-run |

## 9. Five questions a panel may ask (with short answers)

1. **Why not use `time.time()`?**
   Because we replay recordings. A 45-second clip can take four minutes to process on CPU, so a
   wall clock would report a 3-second dwell as 4 minutes and the alerts would fire at meaningless
   moments. The video's own clock is the only one that matches what a viewer sees.

2. **Why does the alert need 3 seconds instead of alerting immediately?**
   The raw count crosses the capacity line several times per second — the P3 log shows five
   crossings in one second on the `mid` clip. Without a delay, one real event would produce five
   alerts and the operator would stop trusting them. `ON_DELAY` trades a small delay for
   credibility.

3. **Why does clearing take longer (5 s) than triggering (3 s)?**
   Deliberate asymmetry. A brief dip in a crowd is usually a person walking out of frame, not the
   problem ending. Making the clear slower than the trigger prevents an alert from oscillating
   on and off, which is the most annoying possible behaviour for an operator.

4. **Why two dwell numbers instead of one average?**
   The average hides the worst case. Ten people waiting 8 s each and nine people waiting 2 s plus
   one waiting 60 s can have a similar average, but only the second is a blocked doorway. The
   maximum is the bottleneck signal; the average is the comfort signal.

5. **Why does the heatmap fade?**
   So it describes the recent past. With no fade, the first busy minute would glow for the rest
   of the clip, and a spot that emptied ten minutes ago would still look dangerous. `HEAT_DECAY`
   at 0.995 means heat halves in roughly 140 frames (about 9 s of video at STRIDE 2 and 30 fps).

## 10. Numbers to remember

- Dwell: keyed by **`(zone, track_id)`**; forget after **`TRACK_GRACE = 2.0 s`** unseen.
- Heat: `float32`, fade **0.995** per frame, blur **0.05 × width** (33 px at 640), mask at
  **8%** of the peak, overlay **alpha 0.5**.
- Alert: fire after **`ON_DELAY = 3.0 s`** continuously over; clear after
  **`OFF_DELAY = 5.0 s`** continuously at or under.
- Result: **normal 0 events, mid 0 events, crowded 1 event at video 31.5 s**.
