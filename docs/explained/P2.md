# Step P2 — Demo videos (explained for a beginner)

This note explains every file and function written in step P2, in plain English, with a
tiny number example for each idea. Read it once, then explain it out loud in your own words.

---

## 1. What this step does, in five sentences

1. It finds **three real video clips** — one calm, one medium, one crowded — so the app has
   something honest to analyse.
2. Each clip comes from a **downloaded, openly licensed source** (Pexels License), and every
   source is recorded in `demo_videos/SOURCES.csv`.
3. `scripts/prepare_footage.py` **scans each raw video for cuts**, keeps the longest cut-free
   stretch, and trims it to at most 45 seconds.
4. It writes the three final clips to `demo_videos/` at **1280x720**, saves a first/last frame
   preview for each, and **measures the real crowd level** with the same `Detector` the app uses.
5. It writes `demo_videos/expected_results.md`, a table of the measured maximum and median
   people per frame plus the alert behaviour the panel should expect.

We did **not** change `app/` code in this step. P2 is only: **sources in, three measured demo
clips out**.

## 2. Which sources we used, and why

The task asked to prefer Wikimedia Commons or Internet Archive, then Pexels/Pixabay. We
searched all three:

| Source | What we found | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wikimedia Commons | Mostly historical newsreels at 384x288 or similar, with pans and cuts | Too low-resolution / not fixed |
| Internet Archive | Mostly unrelated uploads; licences often unclear per item | Licence could not be confirmed quickly |
| Pexels | Modern 1080p/1440p stock clips, explicit Pexels License on every page | Used |

The Pexels License (https://www.pexels.com/license/) says: free to use, **attribution is not
required**, modification is allowed, and you may not sell unmodified copies or imply that
identifiable people endorse a product. Our use is a non-commercial internship demo, so it
fits.

The three chosen clips:

| Role | Pexels title | Author | Resolution | Length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| normal | Busy Urban Intersection from Above | Scott Precious | 1920x1080 | 24.4 s |
| mid | Shopping Mall Escalator in mall | Amit Patekar | 1920x1080 | 31.1 s |
| crowded | A crowded city street with people walking and cars | Vintage Lenses | 1920x1080 | 120.1 s |

All three are daytime, high-angle, fixed-view shots where full bodies are visible.

**Why the crowded clip changed during this step.** The first crowded candidate was a shopping
mall, `15007111`. It passed the max rule (14 people) but failed in practice:

- its **median was only 4**, so it was a normal scene with a few busy seconds, not a crowd;
- its first and last previews differed by **69.6**, because the camera slowly drifted down
  about 9% of the frame height over 30 seconds.

A panel would ask "where is the crowd?" and "why is the camera moving?", so we replaced it
instead of explaining it away. The new clip, `18516076`, is a busy Mexico City street filmed
from a balcony: **median 8 people** in a typical frame, **max 15**, and camera drift of only
**0.2 pixels** over 30 seconds (measured with OpenCV ECC). Different role, better fit.

### 2.1 `demo_videos/SOURCES.csv` — the licence record

Every row answers the question "where did this file come from, and may we use it?":

| Column | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `title` | `Busy Urban Intersection from Above` | The name on the Pexels page |
| `page_url` | `https://www.pexels.com/video/...-36304379/` | The page a human can open and check |
| `direct_file_url` | `https://videos.pexels.com/video-files/...mp4` | The exact MP4 that was downloaded |
| `author` | `Scott Precious` | The creator Pexels names |
| `licence_name` | `Pexels License` | The licence shown on the page |
| `licence_url` | `https://www.pexels.com/license/` | Where the licence text lives |
| `original_resolution` | `1920x1080` | Resolution of the uploaded original |
| `downloaded_resolution` | `1920x1080` | Resolution of the file Pexels actually served |
| `duration_seconds` | `120.1` | Length of the downloaded file |

Why two resolution columns? Pexels sometimes serves a smaller file than the uploaded original
(for example a 1440p version of a 4K upload). Recording both avoids a false claim. All three
downloaded files are **1080p**, so well above the "720p or better" rule.

### 2.2 Why the raw files are not in Git

`demo_videos/raw/` holds 10-78 MB downloads and is listed in `.gitignore`. The repo keeps
`SOURCES.csv`, so anyone can download the same files again. The three **final** clips are
committed because the demo needs them without internet.

## 3. `scripts/prepare_footage.py` — the whole file

Run it with:

```powershell
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\prepare_footage.py
```

It reads `demo_videos/raw/`, writes `demo_videos/<role>.mp4`, `demo_videos/previews/*.png` and
`demo_videos/expected_results.md`.

### 3.1 Step settings at the top of the file

These live in the script, not in `app/config.py`, because they are **one-off prep choices**,
not app behaviour:

| Name | Value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `RAW_DIR` | `demo_videos/raw` | Where the downloaded originals sit |
| `PREVIEW_DIR` | `demo_videos/previews` | Where the proof pictures go |
| `MIN_SECONDS` | `20.0` | A clip shorter than this is useless for a demo |
| `MAX_SECONDS` | `45.0` | Keeps the demo short and the files small |
| `CUT_THRESHOLD` | `30.0` | Mean grey difference that counts as a hard cut |
| `COMPARE_WIDTH` | `320` | Frames are compared at this width, which is fast |
| `PREVIEW_DIFF_WARNING` | `35.0` | Warn when first and last frames differ more than this |
| `OUTPUT_WIDTH` | `1280` | Final width; 720p for a 16:9 video |
| `CLIPS` | three pairs | Which raw file plays which role |
| `EXPECTED_RANGE` | normal 0-6, mid 7-14, crowded 12+ | The measured max each role must hit |
| `EXPECTED_ALERT` | three sentences | Plain-English alert behaviour for the table |

**No magic numbers:** every threshold above is named and explained here. If a number changes,
it changes in one place.

### 3.2 `VideoInfo` and `ClipResult` — two small dataclasses

`VideoInfo` holds facts about a raw file:

```python
VideoInfo(fps=25.0, frames=611, width=1920, height=1080, cuts=[])
```

`ClipResult` holds what we measured for a finished clip:

```python
ClipResult(role="normal", source_name="36304379.mp4", start_seconds=0.0,
           end_seconds=24.4, duration_seconds=24.4, width=1280, height=720,
           max_people=4, median_people=2, fits_role=True, preview_diff=18.8)
```

**Why dataclasses:** the table writer and the console printer both need the same fields.
A dataclass keeps them in one named object instead of a long tuple where field 7 and field 8
are easy to mix up.

### 3.3 `small_gray(frame)` — make a cheap comparison copy

```python
scale = COMPARE_WIDTH / frame.shape[1]
size = (COMPARE_WIDTH, max(int(frame.shape[0] * scale), 1))
return cv2.cvtColor(cv2.resize(frame, size), cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
```

**Why:** comparing two full 1920x1080 colour frames for every frame pair would be slow, and
tiny colour changes (a red shirt) would look like a scene change. A small grey copy keeps the
**structure** (walls, escalators) and throws away colour.

**Numeric example:** a 1920x1080 frame becomes 320x180:

```
scale  = 320 / 1920 = 0.1667
height = round(1080 * 0.1667) = 180
size   = (320, 180)
```

A 720p frame becomes 320x180 too, because width drives both numbers.

### 3.4 `frame_diff(previous, current)` — one number per frame pair

```python
return float(np.mean(cv2.absdiff(previous, current)))
```

**Why:** `absdiff` subtracts pixels and throws away the sign, then `mean` averages the whole
image. Same shot → small number. Different shot → large number. That single number is all the
cut detector needs.

**Numeric example:** two frames of the same mall shot score about 3-10 (people moved);
a hard cut to a different room scores 40-90. Our threshold is 30, so the first group passes
and the second is flagged.

### 3.5 `scan_video(video_path)` — read a file once, find cuts

The loop:

```python
ok, frame = capture.read()
current = small_gray(frame)
if previous is not None and frame_diff(previous, current) > CUT_THRESHOLD:
    cuts.append(index)
previous = current
index += 1
```

**Why `previous is not None`:** the first frame has nothing to compare with, so it can never
be a cut. Without that check we would compare frame 0 with "nothing" and crash.

It also returns `fps`, frame count, width and height, and **stops with a clear message** if the
file cannot be opened or reports a broken frame rate (0 or NaN). A wrong frame rate would make
every "seconds" number wrong, so guessing is worse than stopping.

**Measured result:** all three chosen sources had **0 cuts** — each is one continuous shot.
The scan still matters for future footage.

### 3.6 `longest_stretch(cuts, total_frames)` — pick the biggest piece

```python
bounds = [0] + cuts + [total_frames]
pairs = list(zip(bounds[:-1], bounds[1:]))
return max(pairs, key=lambda pair: pair[1] - pair[0])
```

**Numeric example:** cuts at frames 300 and 500, total 900:

```
stretches: 0-300 (300 frames), 300-500 (200), 500-900 (400)
longest  : (500, 900)
```

Why not simply use the whole video? Because a cut in the middle would make people "teleport"
and break tracking. One continuous shot keeps IDs meaningful.

### 3.7 `trim_to_limit(info, start, end)` — the 20-45 second rule

```python
end = min(end, start + int(MAX_SECONDS * info.fps))
seconds = (end - start) / info.fps
if seconds < MIN_SECONDS:
    raise SystemExit(...)
return start, end
```

**Numeric example 1 (too long):** fps 30, start 0, end 2000 → `int(45 * 30) = 1350`, so the
clip becomes frames 0-1350 = **45.0 s**.

**Numeric example 2 (too short):** a 15 s stretch with `MIN_SECONDS = 20` stops the script
with a message telling us to replace the source. It never silently ships a clip that is too
short.

All three final clips are between 24.4 s and 45.0 s, so no trimming was needed beyond keeping
the whole shot. The crowded source is 120 s long, so `trim_to_limit` cut it at exactly 45 s —
that is the one place this rule actually fires.

### 3.8 `write_clip(source, info, start, end, destination)` — copy frames into a new MP4

It opens the source, jumps to `start`, and reads exactly `end - start` frames. Each frame is
resized with `resize_frame(frame, OUTPUT_WIDTH)` — the same helper from P1 — and written with
the `mp4v` codec.

**Numeric example:** 1920x1080 at width 1280 → height 720, so 611 frames become **1280x720**.

Why `mp4v`? It is the MP4 codec OpenCV ships with on Windows. H.264 (`avc1`) needs the
OpenH264 DLL, which is not part of the fixed stack, so `mp4v` is the honest choice.

### 3.9 `save_previews(clip_path, role)` — the "did the camera move?" check

It reads frame 0 and frame `total - 1`, saves them as `previews/<role>_first.png` and
`<role>_last.png`, and returns the same `frame_diff` score.

**Why:** if the camera panned, zoomed or a cut slipped past the scan, the first and last
pictures look unrelated. A high score is a warning, not a failure — busy scenes also score
high because every shopper moved.

**Measured scores** (warning above 35):

| Clip | Score | What it meant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| normal | 18.8 | Below the line, no warning |
| mid | 35.4 | Just above; previews show the same fixed view, the escalator crowd moved |
| crowded | 14.5 | Below the line, no warning |

The first crowded candidate (`15007111`) scored **69.6** here, and the previews confirmed a
slow downward camera drift of roughly 50-58 pixels at 960-pixel width over 30 seconds. That is
why it was replaced: this preview score is exactly the safety net that caught it. The clip we
now use scores 14.5, and an independent OpenCV ECC check measured **0.2 pixels** of camera
movement over 30 seconds — effectively a tripod shot.

**Why the score is only a warning:** it is raw pixel change, so a dense moving crowd raises it
even with a perfectly still camera. That is why the script prints a warning instead of refusing
the file, and why we then look at the two PNGs by eye.

### 3.10 `measure_people(clip_path, detector)` — the numbers that decide the role

```python
if not capture.grab():
    break
if index % config.STRIDE == 0:
    ok, frame = capture.retrieve()
    people = detector.track(resize_frame(frame, config.FRAME_WIDTH))
    counts.append(len(people))
```

It uses the **real** `Detector` from `app/vision.py`: YOLOv8 nano + ByteTrack, confidence
0.35, frames resized to 640 wide, every second frame. That matters — the table must describe
what the app will actually see, not a different model.

**Numeric example:** 611 frames at `STRIDE = 2` → frames 0, 2, 4, ... 610 → **306 analysed
frames**. If the counts were `[2, 3, 4, 3]`:

```
max    = 4
median = 3   (sorted [2,3,3,4], middle values 3 and 3)
```

Max answers "how busy does it get for one moment?". Median answers "how busy is a typical
frame?". The task uses max to assign the role, and median to show whether the scene is busy
all the time or only in a burst.

### 3.11 `fits_role(role, max_people)` — the pass/fail check

```python
low, high = EXPECTED_RANGE[role]
```

**Numeric examples:**

```
normal  max 4  -> 0 <= 4 <= 6      -> fits
mid     max 12 -> 7 <= 12 <= 14    -> fits
crowded max 15 -> 15 >= 12         -> fits
```

If a clip missed, `main()` prints `WARNING: these clips do not fit their role` and **nothing is
renamed**. The task said never rename silently, so the script only reports.

### 3.12 `prepare_clip(role, source_name, detector)` — the per-clip pipeline

It glues the functions together in the order that matters:

```
scan_video -> longest_stretch -> trim_to_limit -> write_clip
          -> save_previews -> detector.reset() -> measure_people -> ClipResult
```

**Why `detector.reset()` before measuring:** ByteTrack numbers people 1, 2, 3 ... and keeps
counting. Without a reset, the crowded clip would start at ID 300+ after the previous clip,
which makes the console output confusing. Reset restarts IDs at 1.

### 3.13 `print_result`, `write_expected_results`, `main()`

`print_result` prints one line per clip:

```
normal    24.4s  1280x720  max  4  median  2  fits  (source 36304379.mp4, 0.0-24.4s)
```

`write_expected_results` builds the Markdown table and the per-clip details. **Why a file and
not just console output:** the panel can read the table before the demo, and the numbers are
versioned with the code.

`main()` creates one `Detector` (loading the model once), prepares the three clips, prints
them, and writes the table. Reusing one detector is the same speed lesson as P1: model loading
is expensive, so do it once.

## 4. The measured results (what the app will really see)

`demo_videos/expected_results.md` is the source of truth. Summary:

| Clip | Length | Max people | Median people | Fits | Expected alert |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `normal.mp4` | 24.4 s | 4 | 2 | yes | none |
| `mid.mp4` | 31.1 s | 12 | 5 | yes | maybe |
| `crowded.mp4` | 45.0 s | 15 | 8 | yes | yes |

**How to read this honestly:**

- **normal:** 4 people at the busiest moment, 2 in a typical frame. Comfortably under a small
  zone's capacity, so no alert. This is the control case.
- **mid:** 12 at the busiest moment, but only 5 typically. It "sometimes gathers" — a
  low-capacity zone will fill up for a few seconds, so an alert may appear. That is the
  intended middle case.
- **crowded:** 15 at the busiest moment and **8 in a typical frame**, so the alert stays on
  for most of the clip with short dips. That is the case the panel needs: a scene that is
  genuinely crowded, not one busy moment.

Both crowded numbers matter. Max 15 alone can be a single lucky frame; median 8 proves the
street is busy the whole 45 seconds. That is why the first candidate (`15007111`, max 14 but
median 4) was not good enough and was replaced.

One honest note about the numbers moving: YOLO on a CPU is not bit-exact between runs, and the
crowded clip's earlier quick sample (one frame every 2 s) reported max 14. The final table uses
every 2nd frame of the 45 s clip and reports max 15, median 8. Small differences like this are
normal; the role decision (crowded) never changed.

## 5. `outputs/step1_*.mp4` — the visible proof

After preparing the clips we ran the P1 script on each final clip:

```powershell
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\demo_step1.py demo_videos\normal.mp4
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\demo_step1.py demo_videos\mid.mp4
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\demo_step1.py demo_videos\crowded.mp4
```

| Clip | Frames analysed | Avg people | Unique IDs | Avg FPS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| normal | 306 | 1.75 | 12 | 7.0 |
| mid | 467 | 5.42 | 121 | 7.0 |
| crowded | 674 | 8.35 | 72 | 7.2 |

Why `unique IDs` is larger than `avg people`: ByteTrack gives a **new ID** whenever it loses
someone and finds them again — behind an awning, out of frame, or in a dense crowd. In
`mid.mp4` people constantly enter and leave the escalators, so 121 IDs appear even though only
about 5 are on screen at once. The crowded clip has more people but a **cleaner view**, so it
needs only 72 IDs for 8 people per frame. That contrast is a useful thing to point out: unique
ID count measures **how much the tracker churned**, not how crowded the scene is. The app will
count **people per frame**, not unique IDs, for density.

Why is the average FPS 7.0 while P1's smoke test showed 4.5 FPS? P1's number included the
one-time model warm-up on a 3-second clip, which drags a short average down badly. On these
longer 720p clips the model is already warm, so the steady speed is about 7 FPS on this CPU.
7 FPS is still fine for the demo: the dashboard shows a few updates per second, and `STRIDE=2`
already halves the work. Be honest with the panel: this is CPU-only, no GPU.

The annotated videos are in `outputs/` (Git-ignored). Open `outputs/step1_crowded.mp4` in any
player to see green boxes, IDs and red foot dots on the real crowd.

## 6. Choices we made (and the reason)

| Choice | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Pexels instead of Wikimedia/Archive | Confirmed licence, modern 1080p+, fixed camera |
| `36304379` for normal | Fixed high angle, max 4, median 2 — never crowded |
| `14393753` for mid | Mall escalator, max 12, median 5 — gathers in bursts |
| `18516076` for crowded | Fixed balcony view, max 15, median 8 — crowded the whole time |
| Rejected `15007111` for crowded | Max 14 but median 4, and a slow camera drift (preview 69.6) |
| 1280x720 output | Meets "720p or better" and keeps files manageable |
| Whole shot, not a sub-clip | No cuts; two sources are already under 45 s |
| `mp4v` codec | Only MP4 encoder available without adding ffmpeg/OpenH264 |
| Raw files Git-ignored | 10-78 MB each; `SOURCES.csv` lets anyone re-download |

**File sizes of the final clips:** normal 11.7 MB, mid 47.7 MB, crowded 27.9 MB — 87 MB total,
all under GitHub's 100 MB per-file limit. The mid clip is the heaviest even though it is
shorter than the crowded one: MPEG-4 Part 2 spends a lot of bits on the escalator's moving
handrails and reflective glass. If you ever need it smaller, the honest fix is a shorter
stretch or a different encoder, not a lower resolution.

## 8. What could go wrong (and the fix)

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Missing raw video` | `demo_videos/raw/` is empty (it is Git-ignored) | Download the three files using `SOURCES.csv` |
| `Cannot open raw video` | Corrupt download or wrong extension | Re-download; check the file opens in a player |
| `Video has no usable frame rate` | Broken metadata | Use a different source file |
| `Longest cut-free stretch is only ...` | Source is one short shot under 20 s | Replace the source |
| Preview warning | Camera move or a very busy scene | Open the two PNGs; if the background moved, replace the clip |
| `Cannot write clip` | A player is holding the old file | Close the player and re-run |
| The table reports **NO** | The clip does not fit its role | Tell the user the real numbers, then replace or rename openly |

## 9. Five questions a panel may ask (with short answers)

1. **How do you know these videos are legal to use?**
   Each is on Pexels under the Pexels License, which allows free use without attribution.
   `SOURCES.csv` records the page, author, licence name and licence URL for every file.

2. **How did you make sure there are no cuts?**
   `scan_video` compares every pair of neighbouring frames after shrinking them to 320 px
   grey. A hard cut pushes the average difference above 30; all three sources scored 0 cuts.
   We still keep the scan so future footage is checked automatically.

3. **Why did you replace a clip in the middle of the step?**
   The first crowded candidate passed the "max 14" rule but its typical frame only had 4
   people, and its camera drifted downward. The preview check flagged it (69.6), we looked at
   the images, and we swapped in a busier street clip with median 8 and almost zero drift.
   That is the check doing its job, and we never renamed a role to hide it.

4. **What does "max 15, median 8" mean for the alert?**
   A capacity of, say, 10 is exceeded almost all the time (median 8 is close, max 15 is well
   over), so the alert fires and stays on, with short dips when the crowd thins.
   `ON_DELAY` and `OFF_DELAY` in `app/config.py` will smooth those dips later.

5. **Why is the mid clip bigger than the crowded one?**
   Both are 1280x720 `mp4v`. The escalator clip is long and full of moving handrails and
   reflective glass, which the simple codec cannot compress well. H.264 would be smaller, but
   that needs an extra encoder outside the fixed stack. The raw downloads stay out of Git, so
   only the three final clips are stored.
